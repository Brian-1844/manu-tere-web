# Manu Tere

A voyaging bird that takes visitors around the islands of French Polynesia. It plays in English, French, Japanese or Chinese, with Reo Māʻohi words throughout, and is a sister app to Manu Ora.

**Prototype, version 0.79.** Twenty-six islands: all five archipelagos and, at the very end, Rapa Nui. The archipelagos: the Society Islands (Tahiti, Moʻorea, Porapora / Bora Bora, Tahaʻa, Raiatea, Huahine, Maupiti, Tetiaroa, Maiʻao), the Tuamotu (Rangiroa, Fakarava, Tikehau), the Marquesas (Nuku Hiva, Ua Pou, Hiva Oa, Fatu Hiva), the Australs (Rurutu, Rimatara, Tubuai, Raivavae, Rapa) and the Gambier (Mangareva, Aukena, Akamaru, Taravai). A star-navigation voyage between them, a canoe to build in five stages and to equip with five gifts of the islands, sixty-six activities, fourteen legends, a faʻaʻapu with an earth-oven kitchen, a monoʻi workshop and four crafts, a passport and a word list.

## Put it online (GitHub Pages)

1. On GitHub, create a new public repository named `manu-tere-web` (only the built files; the sources stay in a private repository).
2. Use **Add file › Upload files** to upload every file from this folder: `index.html`, `lang-fr.js`, `lang-ja.js`, `lang-zh.js`, `sw.js`, `manifest.webmanifest`, `icon.svg`, `icon-192.png`, `icon-512.png`, `README.md` and, since 0.79, `privacy.html` (the privacy policy page for Google Play; its address is `https://brian-1844.github.io/manu-tere-web/privacy.html`) (ten files since 0.30; the three `lang-` files are the translations, loaded only by players who choose that language), **and, since 0.69, the `fonts` folder** (drag the whole folder onto the upload page so GitHub keeps it as `fonts/`: ten small `.woff2` files and the two font licences). Then click **Commit**.
3. Open **Settings › Pages**. Under Source, choose **Deploy from a branch**, then branch `main`, folder `/ (root)`, and click **Save**.
4. After a minute or two the app is live at `https://brian-1844.github.io/manu-tere-web/`.

`sw.js` lets the installed app open without a connection. It always fetches the newest page first, so an update shows after a reload. When you publish a new version, change `VERSION` at the top of `sw.js`.

## What's inside

- **Languages:** English, Français, 日本語 and 中文 (simplified). The language is chosen on the welcome screen, and can be changed at any time with the globe button at the top. On first launch the app picks the phone's language if it is one of these four. Reo Māʻohi words always stay in Reo Māʻohi; their meanings are translated. The translations live in `19-i18n-data.js` (in the build, inside `index.html`); anything not yet translated shows in English.

- **Arrival:** after the welcome screen, a twin-engine long-haul jet (drawn after the Boeing 787-9 profile, with an original Manu Tere livery) approaches Faʻaʻā at sunset. It lowers its landing gear, flares, touches down with tyre smoke and rolls out past the control tower to the terminal. A Skip button jumps straight to Tahiti.

- **Felling in the faʻaʻapu, planks at the shipyard (0.47):** felling is redrawn from the old accounts, in six steps: ask Tāne's leave with an offering of red feathers, clear the roots, choose the side of the main branch, cut a groove all around the trunk with the toʻi (a long basalt blade lashed to a bent handle, sharpened on wet sandstone when it gets dull), keep silent as the tree cracks and falls, then cut off the branches. The log goes to the shipyard, where the new **Make planks** task heats one end until it cracks, drives in hardwood wedges, adzes the plank and bores the holes with a bone point. ʻUru gum is now collected in the faʻaʻapu. The Next pill no longer announces a tree ready to fell. While sailing, notes and warnings show for about two seconds and fold by themselves; a tap opens them again. The Maeva fish trap ends in a close-up of the pen, with slower fish, large tap areas, pale striped small fish, and a glowing hint after a few seconds.
- **Canoes redrawn (0.48):** the five canoes are redrawn from the Cook-voyage descriptions and drawings of Society Islands canoes, at sea and at the shipyard. The lower hull is a hollowed log with planks sewn on above it (seams and paired stitches show); the outrigger float of pūrau hangs from two curved booms with forked pegs and lashings. The sail is the Society Islands sail, tall and narrow, pointed at the top and curved along its back edge, plaited pandanus mats sewn in panels in a wooden frame, with feathers at the masthead (the crab-claw sail, which is Hawaiian, is gone). The tipairua becomes a planked double canoe with a low head, a board projecting at the prow and a high carved stern; the pahi has a full deck, a mast in each hull and a thatched fare; the pahi tere has the highest sterns, crowned with tiʻi and feathers, red feather pennants on both sails and provisions lashed on deck. Every canoe now carries a bailer and a long steering paddle. On the beach the big canoes rest under a canoe shed, sails furled.
- **Sailing reworked (0.49):** the manu now sails with you, seen from behind like the canoe, looking ahead over the bow; it turns its head to the side you must steer to, toward whatever surfaces beside the canoe, up to the sky when a star must be chosen, and round to you when you tap it. The sea is drawn as rolling bands of swell that travel toward you with crest highlights and shadows; the canoe lifts when a swell passes under the stern and the water in front of the hull rises and falls with it; whitecaps streak the crests in a big sea; a column of glitter dances under the sun by day and under the moon by night (the moon is drawn at its real phase). Each crossing has a wind side: the feather pennants stream to leeward and flutter, the canoe heels under sail and more in a gust, and the wavelets drift downwind. Squalls follow the sailors' accounts: a dark cloud with a roll cloud along its leading edge comes in from the wind side, the gust front hits first, then slanting rain in three depths with splashes on the water, lightning that lights the cloud from inside, both sails down on a pahi, a grey sea. A soft wash of surf, wind in the gusts and rain in the squall are heard. A new encounter on long crossings: the maʻo parata, the oceanic whitetip shark, cruising beside the canoe with its white-tipped fin out of the water; the orcas are redrawn with their markings, and the spinner dolphins get their three-tone pattern.
- **The manu looks around (0.50):** on the beach, the manu no longer always turns right and tips its head back. It inspects the shore like a bird, by turning its whole body: it peers to the right, turns to the left, then faces you (a new front view, both eyes and the beak toward you). Every change of direction, walking too, is now a quick turn instead of an instant flip.
- **Tetiaroa, the shore and the shells (0.51):** Tahuna Iti works again (the birds were hidden in a corner of the picture) and is redrawn: puʻatea trees and tahinu shrubs, each seabird sitting where it really nests, and the hint teaches where to look. Te honu is remade after Te mana o te moana's turtle monitoring and the turtle-watching code of conduct: a night patrol with a red torch to read three tracks (a nest, a false crawl, a turtle still on the beach; white light makes her turn back), watching a female nest stage by stage at the right distance (15 m while she crawls and digs her body pit, 2 m behind her at the egg chamber, 1 m while she lays, 3 m while she covers), recording the nest like a volunteer, and two months later switching off the lights so the hatchlings find the sea, without touching them. On the beach the manu now lives among the plants: it hops over a low bush, never walks through a trunk (it looks up and turns back, or flutters over), visits a flower bush to sniff it, rests in the shade of a tree, and leaves three-toed footprints in the sand. The pahua (Tridacna maxima) is redrawn with its fluted, zigzag shell and velvet mantle, and the Aukena clams close when your shadow passes; the pōreho are redrawn: the gold-ringer cowry with its orange ring and the knobby, six-sided money cowry.
- **A smaller Next pill (0.52):** the pill under Activities now shows only its word (Next or Done) with a small arrow. Tap it to read the phrase, tap the phrase to go there, tap the word to fold it; it folds by itself after 10 seconds, and opens on its own for 10 seconds when the next step changes.
- **Tahaʻa redrawn (0.53):** the ahimāʻa is redrawn: a pit about two metres wide lined with stacked stones, branches and fibrous coconut husks, livelier flames and sparks, porous volcanic stones that turn from grey to glowing red, real banana leaves with midrib, veins and torn edges, parcels tied with pūrau bark, then the covering of leaves, wet copra sacks and a mound of sand, with steam escaping at the seams. The vanilla shade house has its posts and wires, a zig-zag vine with fleshy leaves and aerial roots, and Tahitian vanilla flowers (narrow pale sepals, a frilled trumpet lip with a yellow throat); a close-up shows the bamboo sliver lifting the rostellum and the pollen pressed onto the stigma; the pods grow short and plump, in bunches. The vanilla is now cured as on Tahaʻa and Raiatea: rinsed, spread on cotton sheets (faraoti) in the morning sun, tied up in the sheets and put in a crate at night; the pods turn from green to brown, wrinkle, and end glossy after massaging.
- **Hoe vaʻa, fāfā piti, quieter sailing (0.54):** while sailing, a round eye button at the bottom right hides all the notes so only the sea, the sky and the canoe remain (the squall card stays). Hoe vaʻa is remade: you are seat 1 of a six-seat vaʻa and set the pace; the whole crew paddles on the same side, in time with a drum; press the side you paddle on on the beat; the tārē calls Change! about every dozen strokes and haʻapine! (faster) near the line; each stroke turns the bow a little and the pēperu, the steerer at the back, holds the line. Fāfā piti is remade at the Anau cleaning station: approach a reef manta slowly from the side, stop at 3 m, stay still while it circles and the wrasse clean it, photograph its belly when it banks (no flash), and identify it by its spots, as the Manta Trust does.
- **Copra remade (0.55):** the copra of Tikehau follows the real chain: gather the ripe brown coconuts (the green ones are for drinking), split them with the axe, lay the halves flesh-up on a copra dryer whose corrugated-iron roof slides on rails, cover it in time when a shower comes, prise out the flesh with the curved copra knife, dry it until its moisture is under 8 % (the buyer refuses wetter copra), then weigh the jute sack and sell it at 150 XPF a kilo and load it on the schooner for the oil mill in Papeete. Tikehau is one of the six atolls chosen for organic copra.
- **Felling remade (0.56):** no more offering step. The four canoe trees are drawn in detail, each with its own leaves, flowers or fruit and bark: ʻuru with its huge lobed leaves and bumpy fruit, tamanu leaning with fissured dark bark, glossy leaves and white flowers, pūrau with heart-shaped leaves and yellow flowers with a maroon heart, ʻaito with drooping needle-like twigs and small cones. The felling starts by looking closely at the tree (three close-ups). The toʻi is redrawn after a Cook-voyage adze: a black basalt blade bound with plaited coconut-fibre cord on the hooked end of a T-shaft, with a swing trail and flying chips; the cut is a notch on the falling side then a back cut, and the tree sways with each blow, cracks, falls, bounces and sheds leaves. Trimming is now easy: one tap on each big glowing circle, and the limb drops off.
- **Fewer squalls, and a very rare waterspout (0.60):** a squall no longer comes on every long crossing: about four crossings in ten, eight in ten when the weather calls for one (maraʻamu, toʻerau, vero), never on a short calm leg. Once in a great while on a long crossing, a waterspout forms: a grey funnel reaches down from a black cloud to a ring of spray and crosses the course. Steer hard to the other side until it has passed; through it, the hull is swamped and a fifth of the way is lost. (Funnel clouds have been filmed over the lagoon of Raiatea.)
- **Sea life, stars and creaks (0.60):** whales, dolphins, mahi-mahi and marlins now pass behind the canoe and a little further out, never through the hull. The night sky has 540 more faint stars, a few bright coloured ones, and a slow twinkle. Every ten to twenty seconds the hull of sewn planks creaks in the swell, more often in a big sea.
- **Te vaʻa nati remade (0.60):** the sewn canoe of Raivavae under its thatched shelter, on trestles, with its tools. The drill marks are now big pulsing rings; the lashings of sennit cross the seam in clear stitches; the seam is sealed by sliding a finger from bow to stern; the canoe is launched on the lagoon.
- **Hei pūpū remade (0.60):** on motu Vaiamanu you gather eight empty shells of four kinds (white cowry, pink and yellow pūpū, brown dove shell; hermit crabs live in some), sort them by colour into coconut bowls as the mama does, pierce each one with a needle, and string the mama's pattern.
- **The Tubuai garden redrawn (0.60):** carrots with feathery fronds and an orange shoulder showing when ripe, potato plants with white flowers that yellow when the tubers are ready, leeks with a white shank, and cabbages; the crops go into a crate.
- **The marae of Tubuai redrawn (0.60):** marae Hariitaʻata (marae Tamatoa) at Tamatoa, restored by the heritage service: dig out the roots, raise the three fallen slabs, re-lay the paving, and uncover the old cooking pit under the grass, among the taro fields under Mount Taitaʻa.
- **Honey (0.60):** a hive sits in the faʻaʻapu, in the Grow tab. The bees fill its frames from the flowering plants growing in your plots (tiare, ʻaute, tipanie, pitate, kahaia, coconut, pandanus, mango…): the more flowers, the faster, from about 40 minutes down to 12. When the frames are capped: light the smoker, keep only the capped frames, uncap them with the knife, spin the extractor and fill two jars of meri. Taste it, or sweeten a poʻe with it in the kitchen. Honeys of the Australs are light and creamy, with notes of caramel, fruit, wood and spice.
- **Pearls and ʻape (0.60):** a pearl now takes 30 real minutes to form on the pearl line (was 5). Raw ʻape can no longer be tasted from the basket: it makes the mouth itch, so the button says “Cook it first” and opens the kitchen.
- **Amoraʻa ʻōfaʻi remade (0.61):** the stone lifting of Rurutu at the end of the Tere, on the green of Moerai, with the flags of the three villages, the crowd, the ʻōrero and the pahu. The stones are now tall basalt pillars: the children’s stone, the women’s (about 80 kg) and the champions’ (about 160 kg). Choose whether to rub it with monoʻi the old way (a narrower grip), squat and hug it, lift it on the beat of the pahu to the thigh, the chest and the shoulder, then balance it until the other hand lets go.
- **Te ana remade (0.61):** the cave is drawn in depth, with the light of the entrance, layered walls, ringed stalactites, a column, flowstone, rimstone pools, shells in the wall, an old umu pit and a tropicbird nesting at the entrance. Drag the torch, with its soft flickering edge, to find six things; a drop falls from the stalactite now and then. Based on Ana Aeo (the Mitterrand cave), Toarutu and Tupumai.
- **Te tohorā remade (0.61):** detailed humpbacks seen from above (long white flippers with knobbed edges, tubercles, notched flukes), the cliffs and reef of Rurutu, the guide’s boat with its wake. After the engine is off, look under the surface: the mother rests with her flippers spread and the calf rises to breathe, then the quiz.
- **Te ʻura remade (0.62):** a garden of Rimatara by the sea, with coconut palms in flower, a banana plant with its hanging purple flower, a red hibiscus and the little church. Birds fly in with beating wings, perch on the flowers and dip their heads to drink nectar. Tap a bird to raise the binoculars; they focus from blurred to sharp. Then say which bird it is: the ʻura (red cheeks and breast, green back, blue nape), the Rimatara reed warbler, also found only here, or the common myna.
- **Te pōpoi remade (0.62):** Rapa's jagged ridges in the mist above Ahurei bay and its flooded taro fields with stone banks. Pull up the ripe taro (the corm comes up muddy) and replant the tops. Build the earth oven in the right order (hot stones, taro, leaves, earth) and watch it steam. Peel the corms to their lilac-grey flesh, pound them with the stone penu on the wooden board on the beat, wetting your hands when the paste grows stiff, until the lumps are gone. Fold it in tī leaves and let it sour.
- **Morongo Uta remade (0.62):** the ridge in side view with the stone-faced tower cut from the peak, the stepped terraces and the fosses cut across the ridge, tree ferns and pandanus. The manu walks the path, and gusts of wind must be met by crouching. At the top, explore the tower, a house terrace with its hearth and a fosse, find a stone pounder (leave it where it lies), then find the koko in the forest below, now drawn with its pink crown, rose chest band and yellow belly.
- **Bailing fixed (0.62):** fast taps on Bail! were lost on phones, and the button moved when the tips and the info panel changed height. The bail panel is now fixed above the horizon, every tap counts, and holding the button keeps bailing. The water is drawn rising in the hull, each scoop throws a splash over the side, and waves break over the gunwale now and then, so a squall really fills the canoe if you do not bail.
- **Te poe remade (0.63):** the grading room of a Rikitea pearl farm, with the daylight lamp, the sieves, the loupe and the lagoon through the window. Four steps as at the farm: size each pearl through the sieves, name its shape (round, semi-round, drop, button, circled, baroque), turn it under the lamp and grade it A to D by its surface and lustre, then match the jeweller's colour order from a tray of peacock, aubergine, cherry, pistachio, silver and blue-grey pearls.
- **Te pārau remade (0.63):** the engraving workshop of the Collège Saint-Raphaël. Choose the best shell (a black-lip oyster with its dark rim), cut the round blank with the saw, sand it and polish it with blue soap until the nacre shines, choose a motif (manu, honu, fai or tiki), engrave it dot by dot with the micromotor burr, and hang the pendant on its cord.
- **Saint-Michel remade (0.63):** first the façade on the square, white coral stone with the two towers of 1847–48 and "Quis ut Deus" over the door; then the nave, two rows of coral columns under the blue vault, the altar of engraved nacre and black pearls, the crypt of Father Caret. Seven places to find, then the quiz.
- **Auorotini remade (0.63):** the real trail of Mount Duff in side view: the sign at the crossroads, the pines and raspberries, the fork to Mokoto, the ropes (hold to pull up and let go at the top), the overhanging rock passed on the right, the end of the forest, and the narrow ridge taken foothold by foothold; then the islands to name from the summit, 441 m.
- **Onemea remade (0.63):** the bay and the dune of Taravai in section, with the stream and the seabirds. Peg out the trench, trowel down through the three layers (dark soil, charcoal-grey sand, yellow sand with bird bones), record each find in place before lifting it (a pearl-shell hook, an earth oven, basalt flakes, a coral file, worked shell, seabird and rat bones), sieve the sand for the fish bones, and say which layer is the oldest. The adze of the old version is gone: none was found there.
- **The Pass on the map (0.79):** where the Pass applies, the archipelagos beyond the Society Islands are greyed on the map with a small lock, and a tap on them opens the Pass sheet. When the manu has the Tetiaroa gift and the canoe can cross the open sea, the sheet opens once by itself ("Your manu is ready for the open ocean"), then only on demand. **The web edition:** on the public web site the Society Islands are free and the other archipelagos say "Coming soon on Google Play" (no purchase on the web). Saves that already reached an archipelago keep it. In the Claude preview and on a computer (file or localhost) everything stays open.
- **The tōʻere tuned (0.77):** the slit drum's sound was measured against a recording of a tōʻere practice that Brian sent (the rhythms Puarata, Bounty or Tautira, Tiare Taporo and Toma), without using any of it: two warm notes about a third apart, the deep boom of the hollow log under each stroke, almost no high click, and the same fade. The log and its stick are now the red-brown of the wood in the recording.
- **The instruments remade (0.76):** the four instruments are drawn again from museum pieces and makers' descriptions, at every stage of their making. The **ʻukulele** is carved in one piece with a double-horned body, a shaved wooden disc over the cone, a bridge held by the strings, a bent metal tailpiece, twelve frets, eight green strings in four pairs, niho carving and a honu on the body. The **tōʻere** lies on two trestles, carved bands near its ends, the slit along the top and the cone-shaped stick in front. The **pahu** stands on seven feet joined by a ring, with a sharkskin head, a plaited cord and laces in zig-zag wound round the ring, like the pahu brought back by Cook's voyage. The **vivo** has its nodes, burnt motifs and the coconut-fibre wrap. Their sounds are rebuilt too: the ʻukulele rings short and bright like a wood-topped banjo and is strummed down-up-down, very fast; the tōʻere knocks with a hollow note, low in the middle of the slit and high near its ends; the pahu booms deep in the middle of the skin and slaps short near the rim; the vivo breathes into each note with a slow vibrato. The instruments move when you play them in the music room. The Heiva orchestra uses the same new sounds.
- **Makatea remade (0.75):** **Vaitepaua** is now a walk in three stations: the port of Temao from the sea (the loading gangway, the crane footing, the ramp with its rails under the 80 m cliff), the company town going back to the forest (the locomotive, the repair shop, the bakery oven, the director's house, the water tower under the vines), and the Sahara, where you cross the pits by following the stakes and lower a bucket 80 m to the water under the old windmill. **Te pari** is a tall wall of pale pocketed limestone with the sea below: pockets, edges and crimps pump your forearms, you shake out on the jugs, clip the rope at every bolt, come off onto the rope if you push too far, clip the anchor and get lowered. **Te fare** has new steps: clear the ground, raise the ridge pole level with both ends, hold to lash every joint, thatch from the bottom up; the fare itself is drawn again with notched posts, lashings and overlapping panels.
- **Ua Huka remade (0.75):** **ʻIore** opens on the invisible bay of Vaipaeʻe, the cargo ship turning at its mouth and the barge at the quay: Tip walks, sniffs and sits, you throw her ball, seal the load, set four traps, find the rat in the morning and read the ink cards of the tracking tunnels (a crab, a gecko, no rat). **Pihiti** is a real point count: the birds fly in and land in the palms, the pūrau and the breadfruit tree, you look at each through the binoculars and name it (a myna is not counted). **Haka manu**: the cape is woven on its frame, the feathers tied on in pairs, a painted tapa headband and a crest, then the dance on the stone tohua with the drummers and the troupe: wings up, wings down, turn.
- **Te reva remade (0.74):** your manu now really sits in the tandem harness: shoulder, chest and leg straps, a seat under it, goggles, clipped to the front of the instructor. At the door it sits on the edge with its head back and legs tucked; in free fall a small drogue chute trails behind; under the canopy it sits like in a swing, legs dangling, while the instructor holds the toggles; at the end it lifts its legs and slides in on the grass by the runway of Temaʻe. Moʻorea is drawn in much more detail, from above and in relief: the reef and its passes, the turquoise lagoon and the white sand, the motu of the north-west, the two dark bays, the pineapple fields of the old crater, lake Temaʻe and the runway, the villages, the ferry at Vaiʻare, and the mountains standing up (Rotui between the bays, the sharp tooth of Mouaroa, Mouaputa, Tohiveʻa in its hat of cloud), with Tahiti across the channel.
- **Ua Huka, the hidden island of birds (0.73):** see every bird of the bird book and a poem appears in it: Ua Huka opens on the map of the Marquesas (it does not count toward te pū). The last home of the **pihiti**, the ultramarine lorikeet, because the black rat never settled there. Three activities: **ʻIore**, keep the rats out at the wharf of Vaipaeʻe with Tip, a rat-detector dog (send her to sniff each load, reward her when she sits, seal the load, check the trap stations); **Pihiti**, count the birds of a valley with the bird society (the pihiti, the patiʻotiʻo found only here, and the upe); and **Haka manu**, weave a cape of coconut fibre, tie on rows of the feathers you picked up on the islands, add a crest and dance the bird dance. Your manu can then wear the **feather costume**. Two new birds in the bird book: the pihiti and the patiʻotiʻo.
- **The secret of Tahaʻa (0.72):** make all four instruments and learn the three songs of the sky, and the old musician of the music room leaves you a poem. It points to Tahaʻa, where a secret activity opens: **Tautai taora**, the stone fishing. The canoes, decorated with garlands of tiare, go out in a line; your manu is the thrower in the bow. Strike the water with your stone when the leader's flag comes down, all together, to drive the fish toward the beach; then the line closes into a circle, and you drop the screen of coconut leaves wherever the fish rush at a gap. As the guest of honour you spear the first fish, and the catch is shared at the feast. The prizes: **Te pehe iʻa**, the fish song for your pahu (once a day, it makes the fishing good whatever the moon says), and **tiare garlands** for your canoe (switch them on in the shipyard, under Customize).
- **Te reva, a skydive over Moʻorea (0.71):** a bonus activity at Temaʻe (it never holds back the island or te pū). Your manu jumps in tandem with an instructor, as visitors do: a small plane circles the whole island and you find six places the pilot names (Temaʻe airport, Cook’s Bay, Mount Rotui, Opunohu Bay, Mount Tohiveʻa and the motu of Tiahura) on Moʻorea seen from above, drawn from its real coastline. Then the door opens at 3,000 m: about 40 seconds of free fall where you keep the arch against the gusts, the canopy opens at 1,500 m, you steer against the wind to the circle at Temaʻe and flare just before the ground.
- **Pai (0.71):** a new dish in the kitchen once you know the ahimāʻa: a sweet turnover filled with banana, pineapple or coconut. Cook the filling (a pod of vanilla if you have one), roll out the short pastry, fold it over, press the edge with a fork and brush it with egg. It bakes at the edge of the oven, where it is not too hot.
- **Makatea (0.70):** a new island in the Tuamotu, open once your manu has become a constellation (it does not count toward te pū). A raised coral atoll with cliffs up to 80 m, mined for phosphate by hand from the 1910s to 1966. Three activities: **Vaitepaua**, a walk with a local guide among the remains of the phosphate years (a locomotive, the crane of Temao, the holes, the bakery oven, the water hole, the workers); **Te pari**, a climb up the limestone cliffs with the island's guide (check harness, helmet and knot, clip the rope at every bolt); and **Te fare**, build your own house: choose a round-ended fare pōteʻe or a rectangular fare hau pape, set the ʻaito posts, raise the ridge pole, lay the pūrau rafters, lash every joint with sennit and thatch the roof with pandanus. Your fare then stands on the island and keeps, inside, the instruments, jewellery and crafts you have made (open it from Makatea or the Passport). Finishing the island gives your manu a green feather of the Makatea fruit dove, found on the trail.

- **Instruments (0.68):** make a Tahitian **ʻukulele** (carve it from one piece of tamanu, bore the cone-shaped hole, glue the thin top, string eight strings of fishing line in four pairs, strum), a **tōʻere** (hollow the log, cut the slit, find its high and low voices) and a **pahu** (hollow a section of ʻuru, carve the feet, stretch the skin, lace it with sennit); the vivo was already there. Each needs wood from your shipyard stock.
- **Te ʻupaʻupa, the music room (0.68):** in the garden, play every instrument you made: chords on the ʻukulele, high and low on the tōʻere, the beat on the pahu, five notes on the vivo.
- **Secret songs (0.68):** play an instrument long enough and an old musician gives you a riddle. Do what it says (a wrapped vivo with a feather, a tōʻere of ʻaito, an ʻukulele with the nacre of Rikitea), play the right notes, and the sky answers: the rain song brings showers to the garden, the calm song quiets wind and rain (a storm only softens into rain), the wind song brings a fair trade wind for the next crossing. A song learned is yours; each can be played once a day and holds the weather for one period. The songs are the game's own magic, not real chants, and the game says so.
- **Pearls you collect (0.67):** each pearl is now its own item, drawn at random by the oyster: a colour (black-green, dark black, silver grey, aubergine, pistachio, green, peacock, deep blue), a shape (round, semi-round, drop, button, baroque, circled), a size from the graft (about 9, 11 then 13 mm) and a grade from A to D, with odds inspired by real harvests (round and peacock are rare). The garden shows only the last pearl; the **pearl box** sorts them by rarity, colour or shape, and the bag groups them by kind. Algae and barnacles appear on oysters as they grow: tap to clean them. Pearls from older versions are kept and given traits.
- **Necklace and bracelet (0.67):** the hei poe takes 5 to 9 beads chosen one by one (pearls, and pūpū shells if you like); the new **tāpeʻa** bracelet takes 3 to 5 pearls and your manu wears it on its leg. Every pearl keeps its colour and shape on the manu. A set of round, grade A pearls of one colour is a **matched** strand or bracelet, kept in the Passport.
- **The Poerava (0.67):** a legendary pale pearl that may come, about one time in four, from an oyster's third pearl opened on a full-moon night, if the oyster was cleaned while it grew. Dip it in the lagoon in the pearl box and it vanishes; on your manu it fades whenever it rains. The pearl and its tale are invented for the game, and the game says so.

- **Te moai remade (0.65):** the quarry of Rano Raraku drawn after the slope itself, moai buried to the shoulders and one still lying in the rock face. The moai itself is redrawn with the heavy brow, the long nose and ears, the pursed lips and the thin arms with hands meeting at the belly.
- **Tangata manu remade (0.65):** Orongo on the rim of Rano Kau: the crater lake with its reeds, the low slab houses, the rocks carved with birdmen, the three islets far below. Tap the handholds down the cliff, hold Swim on the pora and let go for the big waves, hold your breath for the sharks, wait on Motu Nui for the manutara to land and lay, find the egg among the stones, climb the islet and shout "Ka varu te puoko", then swim back and climb up with the egg tied to your forehead. - **Rongorongo remade (0.65):** an irregular tablet of dark fluted wood with eight glyph shapes drawn after the real signs (birdman, frigatebird, fish, turtle, plant, hand, lizard, sun). First carve one glyph: hold the obsidian to scratch the outline, then the shark tooth to deepen it. Then read the whole tablet in reverse boustrophedon, turning it at every line.
- **Haka pei remade (0.65):** the grassy cone of Maunga Puʻi at the Tapati festival, with the painted rider on two banana trunks, the crowd and the jury below, and distance markers on the flat. Push three times to launch, lean left or right against the tufts of grass, and after the slope the trunks run on across the flat: the jury measures how far you went.
- **Toromiro remade (0.65):** the CONAF nursery of Mataveri Otai under its shade cloth, Rano Kau behind. Lay the ten stones of the manavai in order (two courses), answer which partner the roots need (rhizobia), plant the seedling and hold to water it inside the green zone; then the seasons pass inside the circle: pinnate leaves, yellow pea flowers, long pods. The tree is drawn after Sophora toromiro, open and crooked.
- **Pupuraga on Akamaru remade (0.64):** the path of coconut palms to Notre-Dame-de-la-Paix, blue and white, with its two different towers that Father Laval wanted to recall Chartres; houses with orange trees, the islet of Mekiro across the water. The song is call and answer: the leader sings the gold notes, and you tap Sing when a red note crosses the mark. The singers sway, and each family comes out with its gift: oranges, breadfruit, a fish.
- **Te tiʻi ʻōfaʻi fixed (0.64):** the young honu of Maupiti was drawn so small that its dots overlapped and could not be tapped. Both honu are now traced at full size, one after the other, and a tap counts whenever it lands near the next dot.
- **The fish of Uturoa redrawn (0.64):** ʻīʻihi with its huge eyes and white-edged fins, ʻōperu with its black gill spot and scutes, paʻati with its beak and pink-edged scales, ʻōura miti from above with its spiny antennae, a school of ature, and ʻaʻahi with its yellow fins and finlets. The stall now has tui, fish strung by the gills on a cord, as they are sold along the roads.
- **Pearls (0.64):** each oyster now gives one pearl an hour (was 30 minutes) and is grafted again with a larger bead: about 9, 11, then 13 mm; after its third pearl it is retired and a young oyster takes its place.
- **Squalls and skies (0.64):** squalls are rarer (about 2 long crossings in 10, 5 in 10 when the weather calls for one, never two voyages in a row). The sky and the sea now change with the archipelago you sail to, by day and by night: the Tuamotu with low clouds that turn turquoise over a lagoon (as navigators read them), the Marquesas with deep blue skies and towering clouds (violet at night), the Australs and the Gambier cooler and greyer with streaks of cloud and white caps. - **Lagoon life redrawn (0.59):** on every island with rays and turtles in the lagoon, a spotted eagle ray now glides with slow wing beats over its shadow, and a green turtle swims along and comes up to breathe (head out, a ring on the water, a puff), then dives. - **Te mago remade (0.59):** at the edge of Fakarava's south pass you first set your reef hook: on a dead rock, never on living coral. Then you breathe with a ring around your manu (hold while it grows, let go while it shrinks); the calmer you are, the closer the wall of grey reef sharks comes, in three depths, all facing into the current, with snappers (and, around the full moon of June and July, spawning groupers). A big shark passes close at the end.
- **Te poe remade (0.59):** haul up a chaplet of drilled oysters from the long line into the boat, wash the seaweed, sponges and little shells off with the water jet, graft an oyster (open it on the wedge, a tiny cut in the gonad, the shell bead, then the graft of mantle against it), watch the layers of nacre build up over 18 months, and pick the grade A pearl at the harvest.
- **Te haka remade (0.59):** dusk on a stone paepae under the needles of Ua Pou, torches, a drummer on the pahu, three dancers and your manu in white feather crowns, with big dancing wings and a clear pose for each move (open the wings, glide, flutter the feathers, dip low).   - **Materials** come from four little tasks, as they did: fell a tree with the adze (ʻuru or tamanu for the hull, pūrau for the outrigger, ʻaito for mast and paddles), braid coconut sennit (nape), plait a pandanus sail mat (ʻie), collect ʻuru gum. Each gives a fact about canoe building.
  - **Customize:** hull wood (ʻuru is lighter and faster, tamanu is stronger in a squall), sail size, a symbol on the sail (tiare, honu, niho, waves, star, manu) in charcoal, mati red or rēʻa yellow, hull paint (oiled, black bands, red and black), a big steering paddle (Polynesian canoes had no keel: the hull and the paddle hold the course), and a rope of black feathers at the peak, as on the Tahitian sail kept at the British Museum.
  - **Provisions:** a crossing of 100 km or more needs about one provision per 100 km: drinking coconuts gathered on the shore, ʻuru from the garden (fermented into mahi), fruit, or dishes from the ahimāʻa.
  - **Squalls:** on long crossings a squall hits. Lower the sail in time (a big sail tears if you are late), then bail to keep the water under the line. A tamanu hull and a pahi fill more slowly; if the hull floods, the current carries you back. The manu handles the squall by itself if it is steering.
- **ʻOri Tahiti, the Heiva rhythm game:** notes fall down a stage at night toward one lane per instrument, as in Guitar Hero: **tōʻere** (slit log drum; red notes are struck at the centre, orange ones near the ends for a higher sound), **faʻatete** (small skin drum), **pahu** (big drum that keeps the pulse) and **ʻukulele** (long notes = hold for the fast Tahitian tremolo strum). Two pieces: an **ʻōteʻa** (drums only, fast) and an **ʻaparima** (ʻukulele, drums and a vivo nose-flute melody), each shaped like a real *pehe*: the faʻatomoraʻa (opening call), the main rhythm, and the toma (ending). The stage is a wooden platform at Toʻata at night, edged with tapa and niho (shark-tooth) borders, with a woven niʻau backdrop, dancers in more skirts and torches; notes are mother-of-pearl discs and the pads are carved wood in earth colours (mati red, rēʻa ochre, ʻautī green, lagoon teal). Each good hit sends a beam of light up the lane, a glow and tiare petals; every 10 in a row the torches flare (“Faʻaitoito!”) and the stage glows warmer as the combo grows. Levels Easy and Heiva, combo, stars, and keys D F J K on a computer. Tap any instrument on the menu to hear it. The sounds are synthesized from each instrument's physics (no recordings yet); real recordings of a Tahitian orchestra can replace them.
- **Sea life on voyages:** from time to time an animal appears beside the canoe, with a fact and a word: **tohorā**, a humpback whale (blow, back, then tail; only from July to November, when they are in the islands), **ʻōuʻa**, spinner dolphins leaping and spinning, **mahimahi** racing beside the hull and leaping, and **haura**, a blue marlin (often called espadon in Tahiti) jumping bill first. One encounter on a short crossing, up to two on a long one.
- **Activities menu:** the pins on the scene are now small icons; the **Activities** button at the top of the scene lists everything on the island (activities, legends, landmarks, the truck, the canoe) with what is done.
- **Voyages:** the bird sails your canoe (a *pahi* once you have built one), a double canoe with a crab-claw sail woven from pandanus, a deck platform with a small shelter, and a steering paddle. The day crossing to Moʻorea follows the sun.
  - **Star path:** on night voyages, a guide star is used while it is low, then the navigator picks the next star rising or setting at the same point of the horizon. Westward, Matariʻi is followed by Pīpiri mā; eastward, ʻAna-mua is followed by Te Matau a Māui. Pick the wrong star and the bird warns that you would miss the island. This is simplified: in reality these stars are not all low on the same night.
  - **Signs of land:** on long crossings, while the island is still out of sight, three signs appear: a cloud that stays piled up over the hidden island, noddy terns flying home at dawn (rarely more than about 65 km from land), and the green glow of a lagoon reflected under the clouds.
- **Market fruit:** redrawn with shading and detail: a banana bunch, a mango, an ʻuru, a coconut, a fish and a tiare.
- **Rewards:** finishing all the activities on an island unlocks an item for the bird: a hei (Tahiti), a pāreu (Moʻorea), sunglasses (Porapora), a vanilla flower (Tahaʻa), a little carved paddle (Raiatea), a red hibiscus (Huahine), a honu pendant (Maupiti), a bone tiki pendant (Nuku Hiva), a flower-stone pendant (Ua Pou), a pahu drum (Hiva Oa), an umu hei (Fatu Hiva), binoculars (Tetiaroa), a pandanus fan (Maiʻao), a mother-of-pearl pendant (Rangiroa), a black pearl (Fakarava), a hinano flower (Tikehau), and a patutiki band.
- **Faʻaʻapu (garden):** six plots. Plants now take 5 to 10 minutes (tiare and ʻaute 5, mango 9, coconut and ʻuru 10), so a visitor can still harvest during a stay.
  - **Marcottage (air layering):** once a woody plant (ʻuru, mango, ʻaute, tiare, mati, tiʻaʻiri) is fully grown, set a marcotte on it in four steps: choose a node, remove the bark and scrape the cambium clean, wrap the wood in damp moss, cover it with a banana leaf and tie it. After about 6 minutes roots fill the moss: cut the branch and plant it, and it starts half-grown. One marcotte per plant. The facts come from the Polynesian agriculture service (ʻuru is multiplied by suckers, marcottes and root cuttings). The real moon speeds up growth while it is waxing. The real season does too: Matariʻi i niʻa, the season of abundance from 20 November, gives more fruit, and the fruit in season each month (ʻuru, mango, pineapple, tiare) grows twice as fast. Food can be "tasted" for a tip on where to try it. Shells wash up on the beaches, and a pearl line in the lagoon opens after Porapora.
- **Kitchen (in the faʻaʻapu, after the Tahaʻa oven):** cook your harvest in your own ahimāʻa: ʻuru, taro with coconut milk, fēʻī, and poʻe vānira (plantain poʻe scented with Tahaʻa vanilla). Ingredients are used only when the oven is opened. Serve the dishes to your manu for a fact about each. Fēʻī and taro can now be planted.
- **Crafts:** a sun-printed pāreu (cotton and a plant dye), a woven tāupoʻo (pandanus, over-and-under weaving), a hei poe (pearls and shells) and a tapa (beat ʻuru bark with the ʻiʻe, then print motifs). The bird wears what you make, and every craft says where to see the real thing.
- **Te raʻi (the star button at the top):** the night sky of the fenua, as in Manu Ora. Tap a star to learn its Reo name and story, play "find the star", and see tonight's moon with its Tahitian night name. Learning all 11 gives the bird a cloak of stars.
- **Surf:** a banded, illustrated wave with a curling lip, a tube, crest foam and whitewash. Catch the swell at the right moment, then ride the pocket by pumping, without getting too close to the lip or too far out on the shoulder.
- **ʻĀʻai (legends):** Hina and the eel (Tahiti), Pai and the pierced mountain (Moʻorea), ʻOro and the rainbow (Porapora), Hiro’s canoe (Huahine), the house of Atea (Ua Pou), Rata’s canoe (Tuamotu), and two garden legends: Ruataʻata and the ʻuru, and Hina in the moon. Each legend gives seeds or materials.
- **Keeping your voyage:** progress is saved on the phone only (localStorage); there are no accounts and nothing is sent anywhere. The welcome screen and **My manu › Save and restore** offer:
  - **Install on my phone:** a real install button on Android and desktop Chrome; on iPhone, instructions for Share › Add to Home Screen.
  - **Backup code:** copy a code (or save it as a file) and paste it back on any phone to restore everything. A reminder appears once a day after real progress if no backup was made in three days.
  - The app also asks the browser to keep its storage (`navigator.storage.persist`).
  - **Living next to Manu Ora (0.19.1):** both apps are served from the same site (brian-1844.github.io). The manifest now gives Manu Tere its own identity (`"id": "/manu-tere/"`, with matching start URL and scope), so a phone no longer mistakes it for Manu Ora when installing; and the service worker only touches its own `manu-tere-` caches, so the two apps no longer erase each other's offline copy.
- **Chemise (island shirt):** dye the cotton, choose two motifs (tiare, ʻaute, ʻuru leaf, ʻautī leaf, fern, honu, niho, waves), a print colour and size, then sew it. Flower motifs use a flower from the basket. The bird wears the shirt.

## Measuring the audience (0.66)

The app can count its visits anonymously with [GoatCounter](https://www.goatcounter.com/), an open-source counter with **no cookies and no personal data**: it keeps a count of page views, of the language used, of each island reached, each activity finished, each crossing, the finale and the installs on a home screen. Visitors are not identified, so no consent banner is needed (in France and French Polynesia the CNIL exempts audience measurement from consent when it stays this limited); it still has to be mentioned in the privacy notice, see below. Nothing is loaded at all until you switch it on:

1. Create an account at [goatcounter.com](https://www.goatcounter.com/signup) and choose a site code, for example `manu-tere`: the dashboard will be at `https://manu-tere.goatcounter.com`. The hosted service is free for non-commercial sites (a commercial site pays about US$15 a month, or you can host the open-source server yourself for free).
2. Open `index.html` in a text editor, find the line `<meta name="manu-tere-stats" content="">` near the top, and put the code between the quotes: `content="manu-tere"`. Upload `index.html` again. (For a self-hosted server put the full URL of its `/count` endpoint instead.)
3. In the app, **My manu** now shows a card "Anonymous statistics" where any player can choose **Do not count my visits**; the browser's Do Not Track and Global Privacy Control settings are honoured too. The preview page on claude.ai has no site code and never counts.
4. In the dashboard, the paths `island/…`, `act/…`, `voyage/…`, `lang/…`, `finale` and `install` are listed as events; the plain page views are under `/`.

Alternatives if you prefer: Cloudflare Web Analytics (free, no cookies, needs a Cloudflare account: paste its beacon script into `index.html` instead), Plausible (paid, hosted in the EU) or a self-hosted Matomo configured as the CNIL recommends. Check the hosting location and retention in the tool's privacy policy before writing the privacy notice.

## Before a public launch

- Have the canoe-building facts checked by a vaʻa builder (the stages are a simplification: tipairua and pahi were different canoe types, not steps of one canoe).
- Have the French, Japanese and Chinese texts reviewed by native speakers, especially the legends and the pronunciation guides (Japanese uses katakana; Chinese keeps the Latin guide).

- Have native speakers check every Reo word, the pronunciation hints and the short phrases. The words are kept together in the `W` list at the top of the script.
- Add recordings by native speakers if you want sound for the words. The computer voice has been removed, so for now the words are written only, with a pronunciation guide.
- Check the travel information (ferry and flight times) with the operators, and add partner links (for example Tahiti Tourisme) if a partnership happens.
- Show sacred places such as Taputapuātea, which is planned for Raiatea, with the guardians' agreement.
- The Arahoho blowhole has been closed at times for cliff safety work: check its status with the Service du Tourisme before promoting a visit.
- Have the Paʻumotu words checked by a speaker (only mago, hoa, kaveu, agi and aria were confirmed in sources), and the Tuamotu and Tetiaroa visiting rules with the Tetiaroa Society, Maiʻao's council and the Fakarava biosphere managers.
- Have the legends checked by a storyteller or the Maison de la Culture. Each legend exists in several versions, and the app says which version it follows.
- **The Pass archipels (0.78):** the game is ready to become an Android app on Google Play. In the app, the Society Islands are free and a one-time purchase, the Pass archipels, opens the other archipelagos; there are no ads and no subscription. In a browser nothing changes. Since 0.79 the public web site is the free Society edition: the other archipelagos show "Coming soon on Google Play".
